Abstract

Vision-language-action (VLA) policies often fail when a robot's executed motion deviates from their commanded action. Such execution errors arise from the robot's mechanics and operating conditions, such as wear and payload changes. We propose self-compensating VLA, a deployment-time adaptation method that enables a VLA policy to pre-compensate for the robot's execution errors when generating commands. Without task rewards or labels, it updates the policy online using the residual between the action commanded by a VLA and the motion executed by the robot. To stress-test VLA robustness across execution conditions that are impractical to cover with physical robots alone, we introduce RoboStress, a controlled simulation benchmark. It combines established joint-level models of friction, backlash, compliance, and gravity-compensation error into seven deployment scenarios whose execution errors depend on the robot's state and motion history. On RoboStress, self-compensating VLA achieves higher average task success than both the base policies and methods that build in robustness during training. On two physical robot arms with different usage histories, it raises the average task success rate by more than 30 percentage points on each arm, and the gains extend to objects not seen in the task demonstrations.

Self-compensating VLA

A frozen VLA with trainable LoRA adapters predicts an action chunk, which the robot executes. The residual ηobs between the commanded and executed motion forms a pseudo-target atarget, which the compensation objective uses to update only the LoRA adapters online during deployment.

Compensation signal. At each executed step, we measure the residual between the executed and commanded end-effector motion, ηobs = Δx − apolicy, from proprioception alone. Because it only requires issued commands and proprioceptive measurements, the residual is a self-supervisory signal available on any deployed robot, with no task rewards, labels, or knowledge of the error sources.

Deployment-time policy adaptation. We build a compensated pseudo-target atarget = apolicy − ηobs and update rank-4 LoRA adapters on the action expert of π0 / π0.5 with a flow-matching objective toward this target, plus an anchor term that limits parameter drift. The adapted policy then generates subsequent commands that pre-compensate for the observed execution errors, while the robot's low-level controller stays unchanged.

RoboStress Benchmark

Evaluating robustness to execution errors ideally requires a fleet of robots with diverse mechanical states and operating conditions, which is impractical and risks hardware damage. RoboStress reproduces these conditions in simulation with established joint-level models of Stribeck friction, gravity-compensation error, backlash, and dynamic compliance. Each noise term is injected at the stage of the control pipeline where it physically arises, so the resulting errors depend on the robot's state and motion history and propagate through the arm's dynamics.

The operational space controller converts end-effector commands into joint torques. Torque-level noise (friction, gravity-compensation error) is added to joint torques, and position-level noise (backlash, compliance) to joint positions before physics integration.

Closer to real execution errors than Gaussian action noise

We replay a pick-and-place demonstration on a Piper arm carrying a 1 kg bowl and fit the Heavy Payload parameters to the resulting end-effector deviations. RoboStress captures the post-grasp rise in deviation and is far closer to the real profile (DTW 0.027) than the Gaussian action noise used in prior robustness evaluations (DTW 0.388).

Real-world Experiments

We evaluate on two AgileX Piper arms with naturally occurring execution errors: a new arm (Robot A) and one used for a year (Robot B). Even the new arm shows a mean normalized command-execution residual of 32.9% (35.0% on the older arm). Tasks 1–3 pick up a gray bowl from different locations and place it on a plate; Task 4 opens a cabinet drawer (25 episodes per task).
